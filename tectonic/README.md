# Tectonic Memory — Laboratory 02

**Discover → Analyze → Present.** An independent, static earthquake study using the entire 23,412-row local historical sample. Open [index.html](index.html) directly or serve the repository with `python -m http.server 8000`; no charting service or network request is needed for the analysis. A web font is optional and falls back to system fonts.

## Sources and dates

- Earthquakes: [Plotly `earthquakes-23k.csv`](https://github.com/plotly/datasets/blob/master/earthquakes-23k.csv), mirrored verbatim in [data/earthquakes.csv](data/earthquakes.csv). The archive spans **2 January 1965–30 December 2016** and has Date, Latitude, Longitude and Magnitude only. The original agency lineage and exact mirror date are not documented; do not treat it as an authoritative or complete seismic catalog.
- Map context: [Natural Earth 1:110m land GeoJSON](https://github.com/nvkelso/natural-earth-vector/blob/693f11422f4e08d2da4566b854dda53eb7c39fb3/geojson/ne_110m_land.geojson), mirrored in [data/ne_110m_land.geojson](data/ne_110m_land.geojson), retrieved **18 October 2026**. Natural Earth vector data are public domain. Land is a backdrop, not tectonic-plate evidence.
- The generated [data/quake-data.js](data/quake-data.js) embeds parsed records, original CSV row numbers, five verbatim sample rows, land geometry and deterministic relay-reference calculations for `file://` compatibility. Rebuild with `python tools/build_quake_data.py`; check exact reproducibility with `python tools/build_quake_data.py --check`.

## What the analysis computes

1. **Data audit.** All 23,412 rows have numeric latitude, longitude and magnitude in allowed ranges. Three Date fields use ISO timestamps instead of the predominant `MM/DD/YYYY` notation; those three are normalized. No source row is dropped. The smallest reported magnitude is 5.5.
2. **Magnitude composition.** Four aligned 52-year bar strips count every row in M5.5–5.9, M6–6.9, M7–7.9 and M8+. Heights are scaled *within* each band; the selected year's exact four counts appear below. The M5.5–5.9 band peaks in **2011 at 506**, while the M7–7.9 band peaks in **2010 at 23**. The complementary cumulative curve counts rows with magnitude ≥ each 0.1 threshold from M5.5 through M9.1 on a logarithmic count axis. There are **738 M7+** and **40 M8+** rows. These are frequencies of *reported magnitudes*, not energy estimates or evidence of changing tectonic rates.
3. **Daily clustering.** The busiest calendar date in this sample is **11 March 2011, with 128 rows**. For each of the three largest records, a separate view counts rows within a 500 km great-circle distance for each day from −14 to +30. Distance is computed with the haversine formula and Earth radius 6,371 km. Seven-day before/after summaries omit day 0. Spatial and temporal proximity alone does not prove an aftershock relationship.
4. **Consecutive strong-day distances.** For each threshold M6, M6.5 or M7, choose the strongest qualifying record on each active calendar date; ties keep the first source row. This avoids inventing within-day order from a date-only file. Compute shortest great-circle distance to the next active day's representative. Six distance bands compare the observed chronology with the **mean of 200 deterministic permutations** of these same locations *within each calendar year* (Python seed `20261018 + threshold × 10`). The displayed 5–95% permutation range describes variation among those 200 reorderings. For M6+, **7,354 records** reduce to **5,561 active days** and **5,560 consecutive pairs**; 370 pairs are under 500 km versus a shuffled mean of 132.8 (range 117–152), or about **2.8×**. Above 15,000 km, 645 observed pairs versus a shuffled mean of 657.4 (range 625–694) are close. The pair explorer shows a selected pair's actual dates, coordinates, time gap, distance and shortest spherical arc. This is a descriptive baseline, not a hypothesis test or a physical stress-transfer model.
5. **Spatial concentration.** Twelve 30° longitude sectors cross six latitude bands equally spaced in `sin(latitude)`, creating 72 equal-area sectors **on a spherical approximation of Earth**. The ten busiest sectors occupy 10/72 = **13.9%** of that surface but contain **61.4%** of catalog records. The linked, separate map draws the actual sector boundaries and every catalog epicentre; selecting a sector emphasizes only its source rows and reports its share of the archive and ratio to the mean sector count. The latitude bands have unequal height in this flat equirectangular projection despite equal spherical area. This describes coarse epicentre concentration, not damage or population risk.
6. **Exploration.** The main map draws every qualifying row, without sampling. A prominent inclusive magnitude slider and M5.5/M6/M7/M8 presets sit above it, alongside end-year and time-window controls. Users can play through years, select a point and inspect the five largest visible events with keyboard-accessible buttons. The sector study is independent of these atlas filters. Both maps use the equirectangular projection: `x=(longitude+180)/360`, `y=(90−latitude)/180`. Dot radius represents magnitude approximately, never energy. The main canvas supports arrow-key selection of the 20 largest visible records. The banner's globe locates the largest 11 March 2011 record and all M8+ catalog epicentres using source coordinates. Its expanding rings and timing are **illustrative**, not measured seismic waves or affected areas. Its March 2011 trace plots **daily catalog counts**, not ground-motion waveform measurements. Motion can be paused and starts paused under reduced-motion settings.

## Limits and interpretation

The file has no depth, magnitude type, agency, event ID, within-day time, uncertainty, damage or population exposure. It begins at M5.5 and its coverage/detection practices may have changed over five decades. Raw counts cannot establish changing tectonic activity or risk. A plotted epicentre is not the full affected area. The map cannot predict earthquakes. A nearby consecutive pair is not automatically an aftershock; a remote pair does not establish triggering. The M6+ day-resolution relay cannot test minute-scale dynamic triggering, which [USGS says can occur at a distance](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/can-a-large-earthquake-trigger-earthquakes-distant-locations-or-other-faults), usually in already susceptible areas. USGS also explains [catalog completeness](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/how-complete-isc-gem-global-earthquake-catalog) and [magnitude versus energy](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/earthquake-magnitude-energy-release-and-shaking-intensity); this lab does not aggregate energy from an untyped magnitude column.
